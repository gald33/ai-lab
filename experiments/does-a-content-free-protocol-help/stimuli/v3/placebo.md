# Placebo treatment — FROZEN

*Appended for the `placebo` cell. Length- and register-matched to
`protocol.md`, domain-neutral like it, and deliberately carrying no convention
about how a distributed group should converse. Its whole job is to separate
"this convention helps" from "being handed a considered-looking document
helps".*

## Some general advice

The following are habits that tend to serve people well when they are working
on something unfamiliar. They are suggestions, not rules. Nothing checks
whether you follow them, and you may depart from them whenever you judge that
better.

1. **Read the whole thing before acting.** Go through the description you have
   been given twice. Details that look incidental on a first pass often are
   not, and re-reading is cheaper than undoing a decision made on a misreading.
2. **Work out what you actually control.** Separate the parts of the situation
   you decide from the parts that are simply given to you. Effort spent on
   things you cannot change is effort not spent on the things you can.
3. **Be concrete.** Where something can be worked out exactly, work it out
   rather than approximating it, and check the result once before relying on
   it. A figure that is nearly right is often worse than one that is obviously
   missing.
4. **Notice your assumptions.** Where you have filled in something you were not
   actually told, be aware that you have done so rather than treating it as
   given. Some of those gaps will matter and some will not.
5. **Keep track of what you have already learned.** Information arrives over
   time and is easy to lose. What you knew earlier is still true unless
   something has changed it.
6. **Decide in time.** A decision made too late helps less than a slightly
   worse one made while it still counts, and time spent deliberating past the
   point of usefulness is time that was available for something else. Notice
   when you have stopped learning anything by thinking further.
7. **Do not over-correct.** One disappointing result is not always evidence
   that an approach was wrong. Distinguish an outcome that went badly through
   luck from one that went badly through a mistake, and change your approach
   for the second rather than the first.
8. **Revisit your approach occasionally.** What made sense at the start may not
   still make sense later, and the beginning is when you knew least.

None of this tells you what to decide. It is only about how to go about
deciding it.
